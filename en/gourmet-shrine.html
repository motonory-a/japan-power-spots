Shinsen: food for the gods, and naorai: sharing it

At shrines, rice, sake, salt, water and food from the sea and mountains are offered to the kami (gods). These offerings are called shinsen. After a festival, people share the offerings in a meal called naorai, the idea being that eating what the gods ate brings a share of their blessing.

02

Shōjin ryōri: Buddhist temple cuisine

Shōjin ryōri is the food of Buddhist monks in training. It uses no meat or fish, and turns tofu, sesame, vegetables, wild plants and yuba (tofu skin) into careful, delicious dishes. Sesame tofu from Mount Koya, yuba from Nikko and Kyoto’s yudofu all grew from this tradition. Some temple lodgings and nearby restaurants serve it.

03

Temple-town treats: a welcome for travelers

For centuries, shops have lined the approaches to great shrines and temples to feed pilgrims who walked a long way. Akafuku mochi and Ise udon near Ise Jingu, Izumo soba near Izumo Taisha: tasting the local food after your visit is a pleasure travelers have enjoyed for generations.

Izumo zenzai

04

Was zenzai born in Izumo?

In the tenth month of the old calendar, the gods of Japan are said to gather at Izumo, so locals call it Kamiari-zuki, "the month with gods". A sweet called jinzai mochi was served at the Kamiari festival, and one theory says its name slowly became "zenzai", the sweet red-bean soup. That is why Izumo calls itself the birthplace of zenzai.

Ajari mochi

05

Umegae mochi of Dazaifu and Ajari mochi of Kyoto

Umegae mochi, sold along the approach to Dazaifu Tenmangu, comes from a legend: an old nun named Jōmyōni brought the exiled scholar Sugawara no Michizane a rice cake on a plum branch. On the 25th of every month, a green version made with mugwort appears. Kyoto’s Ajari mochi is said to take the shape of the hat worn by monks (ajari) training on Mount Hiei.

An-mochi zoni

06

Foods for good luck

Some Japanese foods carry wishes in their names or colors. Tai (sea bream) sounds like medetai, "joyful". The red of azuki beans has long been thought to keep bad luck away, so red-bean rice is cooked for celebrations. Mochi is food for special days, eaten in zōni soup at New Year, and long toshikoshi soba on New Year’s Eve carries a wish for a long life.
