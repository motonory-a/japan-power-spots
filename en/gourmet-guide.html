How to Eat in Japan

Know these and every meal feels easier.
Just ten small customs of the Japanese table.

01

"Itadakimasu" and "Gochisōsama"

Before eating, put your hands together and say "itadakimasu". After eating, say "gochisōsama deshita". Both are words of thanks, to the food itself and to the people who made it. Staff often smile when visitors say them.

02

Two things never to do with chopsticks

Never pass food from your chopsticks to someone else’s, and never stand chopsticks upright in rice. Both copy funeral customs in Japan, so they make people uneasy. To share, put food on a small plate. To rest your chopsticks, use the chopstick rest or lay them across your dish.

03

Slurping noodles is fine

With soba, udon and ramen, slurping is normal. Drawing in air brings out the aroma and cools hot noodles a little. If you are not comfortable slurping, eating quietly with the spoon is perfectly fine too.

04

Ticket machines at noodle shops

If there is a machine by the door, choose your dish there, pay, and hand the ticket to the staff. Many buttons have photos, so you do not need to read Japanese. Some machines take cash only, so keep coins and 1,000-yen notes handy.

05

"Otōshi" at izakaya

At an izakaya (Japanese pub), a small dish may arrive before you order. This is otōshi (called tsukidashi in Kansai). It works like a table charge, and at many places a few hundred yen per person is added to the bill. If you are unsure, ask before ordering.

06

No tipping

Tipping is not a custom in Japanese restaurants. Leave money on the table and staff may even run after you, thinking you forgot it. A simple "gochisōsama deshita" is the best way to say thank you.

07

Street food: stop, then eat

Snacking along shrine approaches and shopping streets is fun, but avoid eating while walking through crowds. Kamakura, for example, has a by-law asking people to avoid eating while walking in crowded places (there is no fine). Eat in front of the shop or on a bench, and return rubbish to the shop or take it with you. Public bins are rare in Japan, so a small bag helps.

08

The oshibori is for your hands

The hot (or, in summer, cold) towel you get when you sit down is for wiping your hands. Wiping your face with it can look a little casual in nicer places. For spills, use a paper napkin or ask the staff.

09

The base of the flavor: dashi and umami

Much of Japanese cooking is built on dashi, a broth made from kombu kelp and dried bonito. In 1908 the chemist Ikeda Kikunae found that the savory taste of kombu broth comes from glutamate, and named it "umami". It is now known as the fifth basic taste, next to sweet, salty, sour and bitter. Dishes that look all-vegetable often contain fish dashi, so vegetarians should ask the restaurant.

10

Small tips for sushi and rice dishes

You can eat nigiri sushi with your hands. Dip the fish, not the rice, lightly in soy sauce. In Japan, people lift the rice bowl to eat (large or heavy bowls can stay on the table). Some dishes, like Nagoya’s hitsumabushi, have a special way to enjoy them: see "How to eat it" in the food guide.
